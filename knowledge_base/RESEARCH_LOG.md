## 2026-11-22 — Boredom and volume: keeping easy kilometres from being boring
**Question (the athlete):** weekly volume is flat partly because running bores him, and he pushes the pace to finish before
boredom sets in. How can he beat boredom without feeding the speed habit?
**Findings:**
- **Direct evidence on boredom and running volume is thin.** Studies that touch boredom and endurance mostly test
  mental fatigue or self-control tasks, often with isometric endpoints and small samples. Treat the remedies below as
  enjoyment and adherence tools, not proven volume boosters.
- **Music** (multilevel meta-analysis, 139 studies, Terry 2020): more positive affect (g = 0.48), lower perceived
  exertion (g = 0.22), better performance (g = 0.31, moderated by tempo). A. Because music raises performance, it can
  raise pace: stories (podcasts, audiobooks) suit easy runs; music suits surges and quality sessions.
- **Temptation bundling** (audiobooks only at the gym): more gym visits in Milkman, Minson & Volpp 2014, an effect that
  faded after a holiday break; 61 % would pay to restrict their audiobooks to the gym. A ~15x larger follow-up
  (Kirgios 2020) found that encouraging bundling raised the likelihood and frequency of gym visits for months. B.
- **Attention:** dissociation (attention away from the body) works best at low to moderate intensity and association at
  high intensity (Lind, Welch & Ekkekakis 2009 review); the wider literature is mixed (Brick, MacIntyre & Campbell 2014). B/C.
- **Groups:** exercising in a cohesive group beat standard classes and home exercise for adherence (Burke 2006
  meta-analysis, 44 studies). B. Social runs can turn into races: pick a conversational-pace group.
- Already logged: variety helps adherence (Sylvester 2016), intervals are rated more enjoyable (Bartlett 2011), doubles
  suit boredom, time and heat (2026-11-21), gamification fades within weeks (Mazeas 2022).
- **The athlete's own data:** easy runs are already easy (median average HR 142; 0 of 46 averaged Zone 2). The push to finish
  sooner shows in long runs: the last two ran at 4:52-4:55/km at TE 4.4, against a 5:22 median at TE 3.5 from Aug 1 to
  Nov 1. Pushing a 17 km run 28 s/km faster saves about 8 minutes. Fitness already took about 19 minutes off a 10 km run
  at 150 bpm in a year (tracker), and the efficiency model's heat-corrected trend is about -7 s/km a month.
**Use:** P42. Proposals for the athlete (not yet planning rules): easy runs by time (long runs stay in km for P8), a story kept
for easy runs, one short second run a week instead of a longer single (P35), one new thing a week (route, strides, a
landmark fartlek with an HR cap, a social run), process rewards for minutes and new routes (T23). Shown on the
November 2026 Deep Audit page.
**Sources:** [Terry 2020, music in exercise and sport (Psychological Bulletin)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31804098/);
[Music and affect, 2025 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41395737/);
[Milkman, Minson & Volpp 2014, temptation bundling](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25843979/);
[Kirgios 2020, teaching temptation bundling](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/TemptationBundling.pdf);
[Wharton on the bundling result fading after a break](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/researchers-used-hunger-games-encourage-healthier-choices/);
[Brick, MacIntyre & Campbell 2014, attentional focus in endurance](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/1750984X.2014.885554);
[Burke 2006, group vs individual exercise (meta-analysis)](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/group-versus-individual-approach-a-meta-analysis-of-the-effective/);

## 2026-11-22 — Deep review: sleep, fuel timing, lifting dose, volume vs the marathon, behaviour change
**Question (the athlete):** an exhaustive rundown of his telemetry and history, the research that applies, and the most
impactful changes that are also sustainable, patterned on his behaviour without reinforcing bad habits
(`reviews/2026-11-deep/`).
**Findings:**
- **Sleep (P37):** reviews rank sleep extension as the intervention with the clearest performance benefit; a 2023
  update suggests adding ~46-113 min for athletes who sleep about 7 h (Bonnar 2018; Cunha 2023). Chronic short sleep
  is linked to higher injury risk (a study of 122 athletes: < 8 h/night, ~1.7x risk; observational). Evening exercise
  does not harm sleep on average; the one caution is vigorous exercise ending <= 1 h before bed (Stutz 2019; later
  meta-analyses: high-intensity exercise 2-4 h before bed does not disrupt sleep).
- **Lifting dose (P39):** more weekly sets keep adding muscle with diminishing returns, and strength gains need far less
  volume (Pelland meta-regression, preprint 2024; Schoenfeld 2017). Heavy and explosive strength work improves running
  economy ~4 % in a few weeks (2017 meta-analysis; high loads >= 80 % 1RM best, 2023 meta-analysis). Foster's monotony
  and strain (1998) linked illness to weeks of high load with little day-to-day variation (observational). The athlete lifts
  4.7 days a week (9 of the last 12 weeks with 5+ lifting days); his running has kept improving, so lifting is not
  hurting performance; the cost is recovery and sleep, not running.
- **Volume and the marathon (P40):** in recreational marathoners, mean weekly km and mean training pace over the 8 weeks
  before a race predict marathon time (Tanda 2011; RMSE ~5 min in the 2:47-3:36 range). The athlete: 41.8 km/week at 5:13/km ->
  ~3:32 by Tanda, against Garmin's VO2max-based 3:15; at 55 km/week ~3:26, at 60 ~3:24. Volume, not speed, is his gap.
- **Behaviour (P41):** if-then plans help goal attainment (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, d = 0.65 overall; physical activity
  g = 0.31, Belanger-Gravel). Gamification raises activity short term (Mazeas 2022, g = 0.42) but fades (g = 0.15 at
  ~14 weeks): rewards must keep changing. Variety improves adherence (Sylvester 2016). Evidence on novelty and
  immediate rewards as adherence tools is thin (studies mostly map barriers: motivation, self-efficacy; Tucker 2025). The athlete's own data: over the 22 days that still
  hold a real plan he skipped none, added 3 small extra runs, extended 3 by ~21 % and shortened 1; intensity add-ons (14 surges for 7, 16 for 8, a
  24-set "light" gym day); the last two long runs were Strava PR efforts. His failure mode is doing more, not less.
**Use:** P37-P41; `scripts/telemetry_review.py` (the rundown any model can regenerate), `docs/DEEP_REVIEW.md`; morning
check: 7-night sleep average; scorecard and Log food: kcal still to eat to reach EA 30 / 45; weekly check-in
(`scripts/checkin.py`); planning rules in CLAUDE.md (plan = ceiling with a stated bonus cap; benchmarks for PR attempts).

## 2026-11-21 — Surge-and-float long runs (physiology, enjoyment, how often)
**Question (the athlete):** the 16 × 1-min surge long run (time-based surge, 650 m distance-based float) turned a dreaded run into an
enjoyable one. Is there evidence for this format, physiologically and psychologically, and how often can he use it?
**Findings:**
- **Floats, not walks:** lactate made in a surge is mostly fuel for working muscle, heart and liver (lactate shuttle, Brooks
  2018 review). Active recovery clears blood lactate faster than rest, fastest near ~80 % of lactate threshold in trained
  runners (Menzies et al. 2010). So an easy-steady float is the right recovery; this is also the logic of coaching
  "alternations" and float recoveries (Canova; coaching sources, no controlled trials).
- **Variable vs steady pace at the same average:** in trained cyclists (Palmer 1999, n = 6) whole-body VO2, HR, energy cost
  and RPE were the same, lactate was higher, and surges recruited more type II fibres (less type I glycogen depletion);
  a 2006 follow-up found no glycogen difference. Small studies; fits P14 (fast-twitch leaning). No study compares a
  surge long run with a steady long run for marathon performance.
- **Durability is the marathon trait the long run builds:** resistance to the decline of economy, threshold and VO2 over
  prolonged exercise (Maunder 2021; Jones 2024, "the fourth dimension"; a 2025 study links durability to marathon results).
  It needs long, sustained duration; surges don't replace it.
- **Enjoyment:** interval running was rated far more enjoyable than continuous running at matched HR, VO2 and energy
  (Bartlett 2011, n = 8: 88 vs 61) despite slightly higher RPE. Meta-analyses: enjoyment equal or higher for intervals
  (Oliveira 2018, small effect, SMD ~0.5); affect right after exercise can favour moderate continuous work (Tavares).
  Individual and modest on average; the athlete's own response is strong.
- **Variety and attention:** a 6-week RCT (Sylvester 2016, n = 121) found more variety → better adherence, through perceived
  variety. Dissociative attention lowers RPE (Morgan & Pollock; Brick et al.). No direct study of "chunking" a long run
  into segments was found.
- **Running with a head cold:** in an RCT with experimental rhinovirus colds, 40 min every other day at 70 % HR reserve
  did not change severity or duration (Weidner 1998; similar 2003). Saturday (84 min, aerobic TE 4.4) was above that dose.
**Use (P36, revised the same night after the athlete's feedback and the lap data):** surge-and-float is the athlete's default long-run
format; the long run that gets done beats the one that gets skipped, and no study says it is worse for the marathon. Lap
data: the last five floats were ~12 s/km faster grade-adjusted (4:51 vs 5:03/km) at about the same HR (161 vs 160) than
the first eight, and at the same surge speed (GAP ~4:27) ground contact was 16 ms shorter and cadence 7 spm higher late:
durability, not fatigue. Guardrails: it counts as a quality session (P15); floats HR <= 165; fewer surges on low-HRV or
symptom days and in recovery weeks (P6); past ~20 km, surges in the first two thirds and the last 3-5 km continuous.
Compare formats with Garmin's post-run effort and feel once T15 reads them.
**Sources:** [Bartlett 2011, interval vs continuous running enjoyment](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2010.545427);
[Oliveira 2018 meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5991400);
[Continuous vs interval cycling, affect and enjoyment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25811234);
[Brooks 2018, lactate metabolism review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29322250/);
[Menzies 2010, active recovery and lactate clearance](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2010.481721);
[Palmer 1999, constant vs variable intensity](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/2498/);
[Jones 2024, physiological resilience](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37606604/);

## 2026-11-21 — Doubles (AM + PM) vs one long run
**Question (the athlete):** pros split an 18K into 9K morning + 9K evening; why can't he?
**Findings:** doubles are routine for elite runners and spread the load (two 6-mile runs cause somewhat less tissue damage
than one 12-mile run); total volume and easy volume predict performance in elite men. Coaching consensus: doubles go on
**easy days to add volume**, alongside a weekly long run that is kept intact; the long run's job is late-run durability
(fuel, heat, gut, form when already tired). "Hitting the wall" is framed as a durability failure, not glycogen alone, and
risk is linked to a longest training run of 20 miles or less. Evidence is mostly coaching articles; no controlled trial
compares a split AM/PM day with one long run in humans (a mouse study found 3 × 10 min = 1 × 30 min for short runs).
**Use:** at 35–45 km/week the athlete doesn't need doubles for volume. AM/PM doubles are fine on easy days (boredom, time, heat) and
count as two easy runs; they do not replace the long run. A same-day split within 90 minutes (short break, refuel) keeps
most of the long-run stimulus and the rulebook counts it as one run (long-run rotation option D). Principle P35.
**Sources:** [Science of Running: singles vs doubles](https://www.scienceofrunning.com/is-9mi-once-better-than-45mi-twice/);
[RunnersConnect: running doubles](https://runnersconnect.net/running-doubles/);
[TrainingPeaks: doubling for mortals](https://trainingpeaks.com/blog/doubling-for-mortals);
[Women's Running: easy doubles](https://www.womensrunning.com/training/the-performance-enhancing-power-of-easy-doubles/?scope=anon);
[Durability and late-race collapse (review)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11901785/);
[Hitting the wall, modelling study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2958805);
[Carbohydrate intake during prolonged running](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32109880/).

## 2026-11-21 — How much history should set pace targets? (HR–speed window, heat, session RPE)
**Question (the athlete):** should the workout generator learn his pace at a given heart rate from a quarter of data and anchor
the next quarter on it, or is that too short?
**Findings:**
- Speed at a fixed heart rate is a valid field marker of endurance adaptation: the HR–running-speed index tracked lab
  changes over 28 weeks in 45 recreational runners (moderate correlations, r ≈ 0.35–0.61), and running speed at 90 % HRmax
  was the most sensitive submaximal marker over 18 weeks (Vesterinen et al.). So "pace at heart rate" is the right thing to learn.
- Submaximal HR falls within the first few weeks of training and keeps changing for months; detraining raises it again
  within 1–2 weeks. Adaptation is continuous, not quarterly.
- Practical retest guidance found: update zones every 6–8 weeks (no source for 4–6), or whenever a device detects a change.
- Impulse-response (Banister) models weight past training with exponential decay; fitness decays slower than fatigue. The
  commonly used ~42-day fitness constant was not confirmed in these results (treat as convention, not evidence).
- Heat: no peer-reviewed per-°C HR slope found; coaching sources give +5–10 bpm at the same pace from ~25 °C and pace
  slowing of ~3–5 % at heat index 28–32 °C and 5–8 % at 33–37 °C (grade C). His own data should set the correction.
- Session RPE (RPE × minutes, Foster) correlates moderately with HR-based TRIMP in runners and separates session types
  better than TRIMP; agreement weakens at high intensity, so use both, not one.
- **The athlete's own data:** outdoor pace at 150 bpm went 5:42 (Jul) → 5:30 → 5:25 → 5:19 → 5:11 (Nov): ~6–8 s/km faster per month.
**Use:** a fixed quarter is too slow for him now. A 13-week window centred ~6 weeks back already lags ~10 s/km, and holding
it for the next quarter adds up to ~20 s/km more. Use a **rolling, recency-weighted window** (half-life ~3 weeks, so most of
the weight sits in the last ~6–8 weeks), recomputed every Sunday, corrected for heat and hills from his own runs, with a
minimum sample and a cap on how far targets move per week. Keep the quarter for the trend and the plateau check.
Garmin's post-workout perceived effort (RPE) becomes session-RPE load beside TRIMP (P34).
**Sources:** [Vesterinen et al., HR–running speed index](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24345970/);
[Vesterinen et al., submaximal running test](https://jyx.jyu.fi/jyx/Record/jyx_123456789_49827);
[Time course of changes in endurance capacity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19346973/);
[Detraining after six weeks of HIIT](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/handle/10919/41009?show=full);
[Retest every 6–8 weeks (RunnersConnect)](https://runnersconnect.net/how-to-calculate-your-lactate-threshold/);
[Impulse-response model review (arXiv 2505.20859)](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20859v1);
[Heat and pace (COROS)](https://coros.com/stories/coros-coaches/c/training-and-racing-in-the-heat);
[Heat and race pace (Outside)](https://www.outsideonline.com/health/running/racing/race-strategy/how-much-does-heat-slow-your-race-pace/?scope=anon);
[sRPE vs TRIMP in recreational runners (2025)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-025-00969-9);
[sRPE in elite endurance athletes](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11155691/).

## 2026-10-11 — Single-run spikes vs. weekly load (injury risk)
**Question:** what predicts running injury: single-session jumps or weekly load/ACWR?
**Finding:** Garmin-RUNSAFE cohort, 5,205 runners, 588,071 sessions, 1,820 injuries. Compared
with sessions no more than 10% longer than the longest run of the previous 30 days, injury
hazard was 1.64× for a 10–30% spike, 1.52× for 30–100% and 2.28× for > 100%. Week-to-week
change and **ACWR showed no meaningful association** with injury in this cohort.
**Use:** P8 (single-run spike check at planning). Note for review: the gate's ACWR veto is a
CLAUDE.md hard gate that the athlete chose; this study is a reason to treat the single-run spike as at
least as important as ACWR, not to drop ACWR.
**Sources:** Frandsen et al., *Br J Sports Med* 2025, doi 10.1136/bjsports-2024-109380
([PMC12421110](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12421110));
summaries: [Physiotutors](https://www.physiotutors.com/?p=26053),
[PhysioExtra](https://physioextra.ca/en/health-tips/running-study-injuries/).
Full text not read; the "1–10% increases also raised risk" claim in one summary is unverified.

## 2026-10-11 — Heavy legs after a long run (the 48 h consult)
**Finding:** lower-body resistance training impairs running economy and performance for up to
48 h (Doma et al. 2017, 2019). Muscle function is down at 24 and 48 h after damaging exercise
(Burt et al. meta-analysis); knee-extension strength recovered by 48 h after downhill running;
damage markers can persist to 72–96 h while running economy is already normal (Paschalis).
No study tested a long run followed by heavy legs directly.
**Use:** P21; gate CONSULT for heavy legs < 48 h after a long/hard run; block heavy legs
< 72 h before a long run.
**Sources:** [Doma 2019 (PMC6548784)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6548784),
[Doma 2017](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/49494/1/49494%20Doma%20et%20al%202017%20accepted.pdf),
[Burt et al.](https://wlv.openrepository.com/items/185bdcd5-5dea-4ff7-a67f-dcdba40a3439),

## 2026-10-11 — Lifting on low-HRV days
**Finding:** in resistance-trained men, lnRMSSD was back to baseline 24 h after six sets to
failure (Flatt 2019) and after varied time-under-tension sessions (Erickson); very high-volume
sessions suppressed some markers longer. Upper-body sessions do cause acute vagal withdrawal,
sometimes more than lower-body (small studies). HRV-guided strength training shows good gains
in early studies; evidence still thin.
**Use:** gate CONSULT for lifting on days HRV option B trips; middle option = cut ~1/3 of sets,
stop 2 reps short of failure.
**Sources:** [Flatt 2019](https://doaj.org/article/7aa301e7f43947f2bd3e2c4ea34c7a87),

## 2026-10-18 — Short daily lifting sessions (frequency, supersets)
**Question:** the athlete wants to lift every day, as hard as possible, in short sessions. Does spreading
the same weekly sets over more, shorter sessions cost strength or size, and do supersets work?
**Finding:** with weekly sets matched, training frequency makes little difference to hypertrophy
(Schoenfeld 2019 meta-analysis; Grgic 2019 review) or strength (Saric 2019: 3 vs 6 days/week similar).
Supersets, drop sets and rest-pause can cut session time about in half at similar volume; they
suit hypertrophy better than max strength (Iversen 2021 narrative review).
**Use:** new P33. Rule 10 format: daily 20–30 min sessions, one heavy main lift with full rest
(the athlete's 2:30 rule), the rest as supersets of non-competing muscles; weekly sets per muscle unchanged.
